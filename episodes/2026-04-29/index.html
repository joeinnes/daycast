King Charles uses his address to Congress to talk about democracy and a volatile world

Source: BBC News

King Charles the Third has become the first British monarch in modern memory to address a joint session of the United States Congress, and he used the speech to put the words "democracy" and "uncertainty" front and centre. The King said the relationship between the two countries was, in his words, more important than ever in times of great uncertainty, and pointedly described the alliance as one that would always defend democracy. Several lines of the speech got particularly warm applause from Democrats and rather quieter responses from the White House side of the room. Coming the day after the state dinner with Donald Trump, the speech is being read in London as a calibrated nudge from a head of state who is constitutionally not supposed to do politics. In Westminster the question this morning is who, exactly, signed off on the script.

The United Arab Emirates is leaving OPEC

Then & Now
Six weeks ago, when Iran was threatening to close the Strait of Hormuz entirely, the question was whether the cartel would hold together; today, one of its biggest producers has just walked out.

Source: BBC News

The United Arab Emirates has confirmed that it is withdrawing from the Organisation of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, the producers' cartel that has effectively set the price of oil for half a century. The BBC's economics editor Faisal Islam frames the move as having little immediate effect on the current Iran-driven blockades, because tanker traffic is the binding constraint right now, but as a profound change for what happens once the war ends. The Emirates was OPEC's third-largest producer in February. Its departure leaves Saudi Arabia much more exposed as the cartel's swing producer, and gives Abu Dhabi the freedom to pump as much as it likes the moment Hormuz reopens. For European energy planners, the practical message is that the cartel's discipline cannot be relied on to put a floor under prices once the crisis eases.

James Comey is charged a second time over a 2025 Instagram post

Source: BBC News

The former director of the Federal Bureau of Investigation, James Comey, has been charged again, this time on the basis that an Instagram post he made in twenty twenty-five — a photograph of seashells arranged into the numbers "eight six forty seven" — amounted to a threat against the life of Donald Trump. The justice department says the numbers, in a particular American slang, can be read as a call to violence against the president. Comey's response is that he is innocent and that he continues to believe in an independent federal judiciary. The case lands at a moment when Washington is still digesting the attempted assassination of the president at the White House Correspondents' dinner three days ago, which gives even a thin charge unusual weight.

Pakistan is accused of striking an Afghan university

Source: BBC News

Pakistani air strikes on the Afghan province of Kunar have killed at least seven people and injured seventy-five, sources have told the BBC. Among the targets, Afghan officials say, was a university campus. Islamabad has not confirmed the strikes. The exchange comes after months of escalating border incidents between Pakistan and the Taliban government in Kabul, and is the most serious cross-border action in some time. The story matters because, until now, the Taliban has had every interest in keeping the Pakistani frontier quiet; striking a university makes that calculation harder to maintain.

Sir Keir Starmer survives a Mandelson inquiry vote, but only just

Follow-up

Source: BBC News

A Conservative motion that would have referred the prime minister's statements to MPs over the Peter Mandelson vetting affair to the Privileges Committee has been defeated. The headline is that Sir Keir Starmer has survived; the more useful detail is that Number Ten reportedly spent considerable political capital keeping Labour members on side, and that the prime minister's former chief of staff has now given evidence describing what he called a dismissive approach to the security vetting itself. The row is therefore not closed; it has simply moved off the order paper.

The Office for National Statistics now expects more deaths than births in the United Kingdom every year from now on

Source: BBC News

The Office for National Statistics has published projections showing that, from this year onwards, the United Kingdom is expected to record more deaths than births every single year for the foreseeable future. The country is still expected to grow, but only because of net migration, and the statisticians have also revised migration sharply downwards. The combined effect is that the population will grow far more slowly than the previous projection assumed, and that natural population change has tipped negative for the first time in modern peacetime. The political consequence is that several long-term plans, on housing, on the National Health Service workforce, on pensions, are now resting on assumptions that no longer hold.

BP profits more than double as the Iran war keeps oil prices high

Follow-up

Source: BBC News

BP has reported that its profits have more than doubled compared with the same quarter last year, with the company specifically pointing to an exceptional performance from its oil trading business. The result puts a number on the pattern listeners heard in Monday's briefing: the Iran-driven price shock is now flowing through to the bottom line of the major energy companies, even as it flows through to household bills. The political problem this creates for the British government is straightforward, which is that windfall taxation arguments will now be very loud indeed, and the Treasury will have to decide whether to revisit the energy profits levy.

A Polish politician wanted at home for corruption has been found living in a flat owned by a Fidesz parliamentary group secretary

Then & Now
Reporting on Orbán's circle moving wealth out of Hungary appeared in the briefing on Monday; today's story is about the inbound traffic — a wanted European politician quietly hosted on the same network.

Source: Telex, citing Direkt36

The Hungarian investigative outlet Direkt36, picked up this morning by Telex, reports that the Polish politician Marcin Romanowski, who is wanted in Poland on corruption charges, has been found living in a flat in Buda. One of the property's owners was hired last autumn by the Fidesz parliamentary group, led by Máté Kocsis, to work as a secretary. Polish prosecutors have been trying to extradite Romanowski for months, and Hungary has refused. The detail that he is living in property tied to the Fidesz group's own staff is the part that turns this from a diplomatic row into a domestic political problem.

Telex stress-tests Magyar Péter's claim that wealth is rapidly leaving Hungary

Follow-up

Source: Telex

Telex's economics correspondent Brückner Gergely has spoken to several Hungarian private wealth managers about the prime-minister-elect Magyar Péter's claim that wealth is being moved out of the country at speed. The wealth managers' assessment is that some of this is preventive communication, designed to deter exit, and that several of the destinations Magyar named are not in fact attractive to Hungary's super-rich. Austria, they say, is too well-connected to Brussels; Saudi Arabia is too closed; Uruguay, on the other hand, is genuinely popular and has a long and slow extradition process. The story matters because the new Tisza government's promised investigations only have something to investigate if the money has not already left, and the answer to that depends entirely on the destination.

A bad week for GitHub gets worse, and Ghostty leaves

Source: Hacker News

GitHub has spent the last few days dealing with a long-running availability problem, and one of its highest-profile open-source projects, the terminal emulator Ghostty, has chosen this moment to announce that it is moving its repository off GitHub entirely. The maintainer's post, which is the lead story on Hacker News this morning, is unusually direct: it argues that GitHub's reliability, security and policy direction have all moved in the wrong direction over the past year, and that the project will be better off self-hosted with a mirror elsewhere. There is a parallel story on Hacker News, called "Before GitHub", which is essentially a meditation on how much of the open-source world's social infrastructure now depends on a single Microsoft-owned platform. Today is the day that question stopped being theoretical.

Localsend offers an open-source, cross-platform alternative to AirDrop

Source: Hacker News

Localsend, an open-source local-first file-sharing tool, is having a moment on Hacker News this morning. It does what AirDrop does — peer-to-peer file transfer over the local network — but works across iOS, Android, macOS, Windows and Linux, and does so without going through any cloud service. The interesting thing for anyone working in the local-first space is the protocol design: Localsend uses HTTPS over the local network with self-signed certificates and mDNS discovery, which is a genuinely pragmatic answer to the cross-platform discovery problem that has haunted this category for years. It is the sort of project that ships because one person was annoyed enough to write it.

A campaign argues that the Android phone is about to stop being yours

Source: Hacker News

The top-voted post on Hacker News this morning is a campaign called "Keep Android Open", which argues that recent regulatory and platform-level moves are about to make sideloading on Android effectively impossible without developer registration tied to government identification. The case the campaign makes is that, in twelve months' time, installing software on a phone you have paid for will require permission from a corporate gatekeeper in a way that is genuinely new for general-purpose computing. The discussion thread is unusually substantive, and the regulatory thread runs back to the European Union's Digital Markets Act and the United States app store ruling earlier this year. For anyone who cares about the right to run software you have written or chosen, this is the consumer-tech story of the week.

F1 says its Miami rule tweaks could "relaunch" the season

Source: motorsport.com

This weekend's Miami Grand Prix is the first race after the regulatory tweaks that the sport agreed during the recent break. Sky's Martin Brundle has gone as far as to say that the changes — which mostly reduce the rewards for lift-and-coast qualifying laps and slightly rebalance overtaking opportunities into the race — could effectively relaunch the twenty twenty-six season. Cadillac, the new American entry, has confirmed a Miami-only livery and is putting the IndyCar driver Colton Herta into Friday practice. McLaren is bringing what its own engineers are quietly calling a new car.
