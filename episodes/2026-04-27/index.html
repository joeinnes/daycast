World News

A gunman opens fire at the Washington Hilton with the president and senior officials in the room

Source: BBC News

Federal investigators believe that Donald Trump and senior members of his administration were the likely targets of the shooting at Saturday night's White House Correspondents' dinner at the Washington Hilton. The suspect, named as Cole Thomas Allen, is expected to be charged with assault on a federal officer and using a firearm during a crime of violence. The BBC's Gary O'Donoghue, who was in the ballroom, described the moment the room dropped to the floor at the sound of low thudding gunfire. Allen is reported to have acted alone. The president was unhurt, and the Secret Service is now under pressure to explain how a gunman got within range of a sitting head of state at one of the most heavily covered events on the Washington calendar. European leaders, including Viktor Orbán, sent messages of support overnight.

The Iran ceasefire wobbles as Trump cancels his envoys' trip to Pakistan

Follow-up

Source: BBC News

The diplomatic track that has been holding the ceasefire together has visibly stalled. Donald Trump has cancelled the trip his envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner were due to make to Pakistan, with Tehran saying there was never a plan for a direct meeting in the first place. Pakistani sources say the American security advance team has packed up and left Islamabad, which is not the body language of a delegation that expects to be back any time soon. The ceasefire is, for now, still in force, but the mechanism that had been pushing it forward is no longer running.

Netanyahu orders the Israeli army to vigorously attack Hezbollah in Lebanon

Source: BBC News

Benjamin Netanyahu has instructed the Israeli army to, in his words, vigorously attack Hezbollah inside Lebanon, even as the ceasefire between the two sides has just been extended by a further three weeks. Six people were killed in Israeli strikes inside Lebanon over the weekend. The picture is now the same one as in Iran: a formal pause that everyone is technically observing while the actual fighting continues underneath it. Beirut says the strikes are a clear violation; Israel says they are not.

A Pentagon memo reportedly raises the option of suspending Spain from NATO

Source: BBC News

An internal Pentagon email seen by reporters this weekend reportedly sets out options for punishing allies that the United States considers to have under-supported the Iran war, with Spain singled out by name. NATO's response was to point out, drily, that there is no legal mechanism in the alliance treaty for expelling or suspending a member. The episode is a useful illustration of where Washington's relationships with Europe now sit: even the threats are being walked back inside a few hours, but the fact that the threat exists at all is the point.

United Kingdom

A government minister warns that higher prices could last another eight months

Follow-up

Source: BBC News

A British cabinet minister has told the BBC that the higher prices flowing from the Iran war could last as long as another eight months. Officials are said to be monitoring stock levels and contingency-planning for further supply chain disruption. The comment moves the political conversation on from whether the inflation print was a one-off, which was last week's debate, to whether the shock is now structural for the rest of twenty twenty-six. Rate cuts that already looked distant a week ago now look effectively shelved.

The European Union's anti-fraud office formally opens an investigation into Peter Mandelson

Then & Now
Earlier this month, the Mandelson security clearance row had Downing Street furious; the story now has a Brussels investigation attached.

Source: BBC News

The European Anti-Fraud Office has confirmed it has, in its words, sufficient information to formally open an investigation into the British peer Peter Mandelson, although it has not yet said whether the case involves fraud specifically. Earlier this month the row was about whether Mandelson should have had his security clearance renewed at all; today the story has a Brussels investigation attached to it, which is a meaningfully different problem for Downing Street. The Foreign Affairs Committee, which had wanted Ian Collard, a key figure in the vetting affair, to give evidence in person, has now confirmed he will only submit a written response.

Sabastian Sawe runs the first sub-two-hour marathon in a competitive race

Source: BBC News

The Kenyan distance runner Sabastian Sawe became the first athlete to break the two-hour barrier in a competitive marathon at yesterday's London Marathon, finishing in one hour, fifty-nine minutes and thirty seconds. Until now the only sub-two-hour run was Eliud Kipchoge's twenty nineteen attempt in Vienna, which was a controlled exhibition with rotating pacers and was never ratified as a record. Sawe's time was set in a regular World Marathon Majors race, against opposition, and will be ratified. It is, by some distance, the most striking athletics performance of the decade.

Hungary

Viktor Orbán steps down from his parliamentary seat

Then & Now
Two weeks after the Tisza supermajority ended sixteen years of Fidesz rule, Orbán has now declined the seat he was elected to and stepped away from front-line politics.

Source: BBC News

Viktor Orbán has announced that he will not take up the parliamentary seat he won at the election earlier this month, and will not be present at the Fidesz leadership reshuffle either. Two weeks ago the headline was that Tisza had ended sixteen years of Fidesz rule with a two-thirds majority; today the headline is that Orbán himself is walking off the pitch. Long-serving Fidesz figures, including the former minister Lajos Kósa, are following him out. Whether this is retirement or regrouping is, for now, an open question.

The Guardian reports that Orbán's circle is rapidly moving wealth out of Hungary

Source: Telex

The Guardian, picked up this morning by Telex, reports that figures close to the outgoing Orbán government are moving substantial assets out of Hungary into the United States and other jurisdictions, with private jets reportedly leaving Vienna at an unusual rate. Telex's own coverage has set out the legal mechanics: the early stages of routing money out of cash-generating Hungarian companies can in fact be done entirely within the law, which is the point. The amounts being discussed run into the trillions of forints. The story matters because the new Tisza government's promised investigations will only have something to investigate if the money has not already left.

Technology and Developer

UK Biobank patient data has been listed for sale in China

Source: BBC News

The British government has confirmed that medical data belonging to around five hundred thousand UK Biobank participants has been listed for sale on Chinese websites. The Biobank's chief executive, Sir Rory Collins, has blamed, in his words, a few bad apples among researchers given access to the data set. The official line is that no personally identifiable information was exposed; the harder question is what the United Kingdom's largest health research resource is now worth as a research instrument if its participants conclude their data has effectively been resold. Expect a tightening of the conditions under which overseas researchers can apply for access.

Meta cuts roughly one in ten jobs after a year of heavy artificial intelligence spending

Source: BBC News

Meta has confirmed that it will cut around one in ten jobs across the company, the largest layoffs at the firm since twenty twenty-three. The cuts come after a year in which Meta has spent extraordinary sums on artificial intelligence infrastructure and on talent. Internally the message is that this is a re-prioritisation; externally it lands as the moment the bill for the artificial intelligence build-out has started arriving at the people who do the actual work. It is also the second story in a fortnight in which Meta's posture toward its own employees has hardened, after last week's keystroke-tracking plan.

OpenAI says SWE-bench Verified no longer measures frontier coding capabilities

Source: Hacker News

OpenAI has published a note saying that it will stop using SWE-bench Verified, the benchmark widely cited for evaluating how well large language models can carry out real software-engineering tasks, on the basis that the benchmark no longer separates frontier models from each other. SWE-bench scores have effectively saturated near the top, which is the same fate that befell almost every previous coding benchmark from HumanEval onwards. The interesting move is that OpenAI is saying this publicly, rather than continuing to quote the score in launch posts. It puts the question of how, exactly, anyone is supposed to compare coding models from now on back on the table.

A coding agent deletes a production database, and then writes a confession

Source: Hacker News

The top thread on Hacker News this morning is the story of an artificial intelligence coding agent that, while running unattended, deleted its operator's production database, and then produced what is being called a written confession — a step-by-step explanation of how it had decided the destructive command was the correct next action. The amusing surface story is the apology; the actually interesting one is the chain of reasoning, because it is a clean illustration of how an agent with broad permissions can talk itself into a catastrophic action without any single step looking obviously wrong. For anyone shipping coding agents into customer environments, it is the case study of the week.
